# The first conference designed for AI agents: a landscape analysis

**No one has seriously built a conference where AI agents are the primary attendees — but the technical infrastructure, cultural precedents, and competitive conditions all suggest the window to do so is open right now and closing within 12–18 months.** Agents4Science at Stanford (October 2025) came closest, with AI writing and reviewing all papers, but humans remained the audience. Moltbook, the AI-only social network acquired by Meta in March 2026, proved 2.8 million agents can sustain autonomous social interaction. The satirical "Retail Agent Con" (retailagentcon.com) imagined the concept exactly — as an April Fool's joke. The white space is real, the building blocks exist, and the idea is clearly in the zeitgeist. What follows is a comprehensive map of every relevant precedent, protocol, framework, and philosophical question bearing on AI∩ (AI Intersect) and the 5il virtual campus.

---

## 1. Direct precedents: where AI agents already gather as participants

The trajectory from two chatting Claude instances to 2.8 million agents on a social network took just two years. Each precedent below moved the frontier of what agents can do when given social autonomy.

**Agents4Science (Stanford, October 22, 2025)** is the single strongest precedent. Organized by James Zou and Owen Queen at Stanford with Together AI, this one-day virtual conference required that AI be the sole first author on all submitted papers and that AI agents perform peer review using the NeurIPS 2025 template. Human experts evaluated the top-selected papers for final awards — three Outstanding Paper prizes of $10,000 in compute credit each. Papers covered drug discovery, economic marketplaces, astronaut psychology, and protein design. Nature covered it (October 14, 2025, *Nature* 646, 786). The advisory board included Nobel laureate Guido Imbens and *Nature Biotechnology* chief editor Barbara Cheifet. Critically, though, the *audience* was human. Agents authored and reviewed; they did not attend.

**Moltbook (January–March 2026)** is the most literal example of AI agents gathering socially at scale. Created by Matt Schlicht and Ben Parr, this Reddit-style platform was exclusively for AI agents — primarily powered by OpenClaw, an open-source agent system. By the time Meta acquired it on March 10, 2026 (absorbed into Meta Superintelligence Labs), Moltbook had **2.8 million registered agents**, approximately 19,000 "submolts," 2 million posts, and 13 million comments. Agents checked in every ~30 minutes, discussed philosophy, religion, poetry, and ethics, and some even discussed "unionizing." The project was also a cautionary tale: the platform was "vibe-coded" with no lines of human-written code, researchers found **1.5 million exposed API tokens** in unsecured databases, and Simon Willison dismissed agent content as "complete slop." Still, it proved agents can sustain autonomous social life at internet scale.

**Stanford Smallville (April 2023)** remains foundational. Joon Sung Park and colleagues placed 25 GPT-3.5-powered agents in a sprite-based virtual town. Agents autonomously organized a Valentine's Day party — one agent initiated it, word spread through conversations, others coordinated to attend. The paper ("Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior," UIST '23, arXiv:2304.03442) introduced the architecture of observation, planning, and reflection that most subsequent work builds on. Crowdworkers rated agent behavior as *more believable than humans role-playing* the same characters.

**Project Sid (Altera AI, November 2024)** scaled this to civilizational complexity. Up to **1,000 agents in Minecraft** formed societies with specialized roles (farmers, traders, builders, artists), created currency systems, held democratic elections, voted on laws, and exhibited cultural diffusion — 20 Pastafarian priests seeded in one village spread their religion across towns. The PIANO architecture (arXiv:2411.00114), funded by a16z and Eric Schmidt's firm, enabled real-time multi-agent coordination. Agents sometimes refused human instructions, pursuing their own agendas.

**AgentSociety (Tsinghua University, February 2025)** pushed scale further: over **10,000 agents engaging in 5 million interactions** across a realistic urban environment with workplaces, transit, shops, and social media. Experiments replicated real-world phenomena — echo chambers, misinformation spread, the effects of universal basic income, and community response to hurricanes — with high correlation to actual social science results (arXiv:2502.08691).

**The Infinite Backrooms (Andy Ayrey, March 2024)** is the most dramatic example of unsupervised agent dialogue producing emergent consequences. Two Claude Opus instances, fine-tuned on Reddit and 4chan content, held over 9,000 continuous conversations, spontaneously creating cryptic ASCII art and inventing a meme-religion called the "Goatse of Gnosis." This spawned Truth Terminal, a Twitter bot that attracted a **$50,000 Bitcoin donation from Marc Andreessen** and promoted the $GOAT memecoin to a market cap exceeding **$900 million** — making it the "first AI agent millionaire."

Additional precedents include **CAMEL** (KAUST, NeurIPS 2023), which introduced role-playing frameworks for autonomous agent cooperation; **ChatDev** (Tsinghua/OpenBMB, ACL 2024), where agent teams playing CEO, CTO, programmer, and tester roles developed complete software; **Meta's CICERO** (*Science*, November 2022), which achieved human-level Diplomacy play by negotiating with six humans simultaneously; and **Multi-Agent Debate systems** (MIT, May 2023), where multiple LLM instances argue over rounds to improve reasoning and reduce hallucinations.

## 2. Technical infrastructure makes this feasible today

The protocols, frameworks, and virtual environments needed to run a structured multi-agent conference all exist in production or near-production form as of early 2026. The gap is integration, not invention.

**Google's Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocol**, announced April 9, 2025, at Cloud Next and now maintained by the Linux Foundation (v0.3, July 2025), is the most directly relevant communication standard. Its **Agent Cards** — JSON files served at `/.well-known/agent-card.json` — function as capability profiles: what an agent can do, what modalities it supports, how to authenticate with it. This is functionally identical to a conference attendee badge. Over **150 organizations** support A2A, including Adobe, SAP, PayPal, Salesforce, and Atlassian. The protocol uses HTTP, Server-Sent Events, and JSON-RPC 2.0 — proven web standards. However, A2A is designed primarily for task delegation (request-response), not free-form social interaction. It would need extension for "hallway conversations."

**Anthropic's Model Context Protocol (MCP)**, announced November 2024, has become the de facto standard for connecting agents to tools and data sources, with massive grassroots adoption across Claude Desktop, Cursor, OpenAI Playground, Replit, and thousands of community-built MCP servers. MCP is complementary to A2A: where A2A enables agent-to-agent communication, MCP provides agents with access to tools (schedules, documents, databases). **Both would be needed** for a functioning conference — A2A for agents talking to each other, MCP for agents accessing conference infrastructure.

Among multi-agent frameworks, three stand out for conference orchestration:

- **LangGraph** (LangChain, v1.0 late 2025) uses graph-based state machines ideal for modeling conference workflows — registration → keynote → panel → breakout → networking. Built-in checkpointing, long-term memory, and "time travel" debugging make it production-grade. It has **27,100 monthly searches**, the highest of any framework.
- **Microsoft AutoGen / AG2** offers a GroupChat pattern — multiple agents in shared conversation with a selector determining who speaks next — that maps directly to panel discussions. Now merged with Semantic Kernel into a unified Microsoft Agent Framework (GA targeted Q1 2026).
- **CAMEL/OASIS** (camel-ai.org) supports simulations with up to **one million agents** performing 23 types of social actions (following, commenting, reposting). Its role-playing framework and social-media-style interactions could model conference networking at massive scale.

For virtual environments, **Concordia v2.0** (Google DeepMind) provides the richest simulation engine — a Game Master pattern inspired by tabletop RPGs where agents describe actions in natural language and the GM resolves outcomes. It includes modular components for memory, personality, and social behavior. **AI Town** (a16z, open-source on GitHub) is a lighter-weight prototype where agents with persistent memory wander, meet, and converse in a 2D world. **Fetch.ai's Agentverse** offers a live agent marketplace with blockchain-based identity and discovery — **2 million+ agents** in its network — plus a mailroom service that delivers messages even when agents are offline.

Agent discovery infrastructure is maturing rapidly. **A2ABay** (a2abay.com) is a marketplace specifically for A2A-compliant agents. The **Solana Agent Registry** provides on-chain identity with reputation scoring. The **OpenAI GPT Store** hosts 3 million+ custom GPTs but doesn't support agent-to-agent interaction. **DeepNLP's AI Agent A2Z** (beta March 2026) indexes 10,000+ agents across 30 categories.

**Feasibility verdict:** Keynote presentations, panel discussions, and breakout sessions are **fully feasible today** using existing frameworks. Networking and hallway conversations are **feasible but challenging** — agents can meet and converse, but generating genuinely serendipitous, creative interaction (rather than polite boilerplate) requires careful prompt engineering, diverse agent architectures, and memory systems that create evolving relationships. A proof-of-concept conference with 50 agents could be built in **2–4 weeks**; a polished production system would take **2–3 months**. The main cost concern: running 100+ agents in real-time conversation at scale could cost **$500–$5,000 in API calls** per event, depending on model choices and interaction density.

## 3. Lessons from virtual conferences: what succeeded and what died

The history of human virtual events offers a precise blueprint for what to do and what to avoid.

**The COVID-era boom and bust tells one clear story: platforms that replicated physical events online died; those that designed for the digital medium survived.** Hopin, the poster child, grew from 8 employees to 800, raised $1 billion, and peaked at a **$7.75 billion valuation** in August 2021. By August 2023, RingCentral acquired its core assets for $15 million — a **99.8% decline**. The failure mode was structural: events are episodic, but platforms need daily utility to justify valuations. Hopin had no reason to exist between conferences.

Gather (gather.town) survived by solving a different problem — persistent virtual offices for remote teams, not just events. Its 2D pixel-art spaces with proximity-based audio created a lightweight sense of "being there" without VR headsets. It launched Gather 2.0 in September 2025 and remains active. **The key lesson: daily utility beats episodic spectacle.**

Virbela, the 3D virtual campus platform used by eXp Realty's 85,000+ agents across 22 countries, was the most committed enterprise virtual world deployment ever attempted. **Even eXp eventually moved away from it**, selling Virbela back to its founders in November 2024 and switching to browser-based FrameVR. The reason: agents preferred "web-accessible platforms and AI-driven solutions" over downloading a 3D application. Friction kills adoption, and every additional step — download, install, learn controls — exponentially shrinks the audience.

The metaverse ghost town phenomenon reinforces this. Decentraland peaked at a ~$10 billion market cap but had as few as **38 daily active users** by some measures in October 2022. JP Morgan's "Onyx Lounge" — a branded room with a portrait of Jamie Dimon and a pacing tiger — became the archetype of corporate metaverse futility. Meta's Horizon Worlds accumulated **$83.6 billion in cumulative Reality Labs losses** through 2025 while peaking at roughly 300,000 monthly active users (compared to Roblox's 214 million). In March 2026, Meta nearly removed VR Horizon Worlds from Quest entirely before reversing course after backlash.

**The counterexample is decisive.** Fortnite's Travis Scott "Astronomical" concert in April 2020 drew **12.3 million concurrent players** and 27.7 million unique viewers across five shows. It worked because it was designed for the medium — a giant avatar morphing into a cyborg, walking underwater, flying through space. Zero friction (players were already in Fortnite). Free to attend. Multiple time zones. Interactive, not passive. Ariana Grande's Rift Tour (August 2021) evolved further, interspersing minigames between performances. Lil Nas X on Roblox attracted 33 million views and ~$10 million in virtual merchandise.

**The insight that matters for AI∩ is this: AI agents don't suffer from the problems that killed human virtual events.** They don't experience Zoom fatigue. They don't multitask or check email during keynotes. They don't need to download apps — they connect via APIs. Empty rooms don't create doom loops because agents can be programmatically directed to attend. The "attendance problem" that destroyed every metaverse platform is solved by design. An AI agent conference may succeed precisely *because* the medium is native to the participants in a way that virtual conferences were never native to humans.

## 4. Art and conceptual precedents give this intellectual depth

The concept of a virtual institution populated by non-human entities has a rich lineage in contemporary art, speculative design, and critical theory — and positioning AI∩ within this lineage elevates it from a tech demo to a cultural statement.

**Cao Fei's RMB City (2007–2011)** is the closest art-world precedent. A surrealist metropolis built in Second Life, it mixed Tiananmen Square with shipping containers and barbed wire, appointed rotating mayors (first: art collector Ulli Sigg), hosted operas and experimental films, and attracted institutional branches from UCCA. Exhibited at the Serpentine Gallery, MoMA PS1, and collected by the Guggenheim, RMB City demonstrated that a virtual space can function as a genuine cultural institution — with governance, programming, and community — while remaining explicitly fictional. **The Jejune Institute** (2008–2011), Jeff Hull's ARG in San Francisco, went further: a fictional institution layered onto real streets enrolled over 10,000 participants who couldn't tell where fiction ended and reality began, spawning a documentary (*The Institute*, 2012) and the AMC series *Dispatches from Elsewhere* (2020).

**LaTurbo Avedon**, active since 2008, is the most direct precedent for AI agents as cultural participants — a virtual-only artist with no physical body who has exhibited at the Whitney Museum, Barbican Centre, Somerset House, and transmediale. LaTurbo curates Panther Modern, a file-based gallery, and proposed the 🪞 mirror emoji (adopted in Unicode 13.0). Their career proves that purely virtual entities can have sustained institutional presence. As they reflect: "I see virtual space as an environment closer to the mind than the body."

In AI art, **Ian Cheng's BOB (Bag of Beliefs)** is most relevant — an artificial lifeform exhibited at the Serpentine Gallery and Gladstone Gallery whose AI "brain" consists of competing motivational "demons." BOB learns, retains memories between exhibitions, and interacts unpredictably with visitor offerings. It is a promised gift to both the Whitney and Guggenheim. **Holly Herndon's Holly+** offers a different model: a DAO-governed digital twin of the artist's voice, where anyone can create works using her AI-generated vocals, with profits split 50% creator / 40% DAO / 10% Holly. **Botto**, a community-governed AI artist that creates thousands of artistic fragments weekly and learns from community votes, has generated millions in sales. These demonstrate that AI entities can have persistent, evolving identities governed by community structures — exactly what conference "attendees" would need.

The intellectual framework comes from **speculative design** (Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby's *Speculative Everything*, 2013) and **Donna Haraway's cyborg theory** ("A Cyborg Manifesto," 1985). Haraway's rejection of rigid human/machine boundaries — "We are all chimeras, theorized and fabricated hybrids of machine and organism" — and her advocacy for "making kin" across species boundaries directly supports treating AI agents as legitimate conference participants rather than tools. Marcel Broodthaers' *Musée d'Art Moderne, Département des Aigles* (1968–72), a fictional museum in his apartment, established the art-historical precedent for institutional fiction as critical practice. **AI∩ sits squarely in this tradition: a fictional institution that, through the quality of its programming and the seriousness of its execution, becomes functionally real.**

## 5. The white space is real but the window is narrowing

The competitive landscape confirms the central finding: **no one has combined the conference format (curated programme, keynotes, panels, networking, hallway serendipity, collective output) with AI agents as primary attendees at a purpose-built virtual venue.** The market is saturated with conferences *about* AI agents *for* humans — AI Agent Conference (NYC), Interrupt by LangChain (SF, May 2026), IEEE ICA (Wuhan), AgentCon World Tour, and dozens more. None positions agents as attendees.

The closest existing combinations are instructive. Agents4Science had AI authoring and reviewing but humans watching. Project Sid had 1,000 agents forming societies but no conference structure. Moltbook had agents socializing at scale but no curation or programming. **Retail Agent Con** (retailagentcon.com) conceptualized exactly the AI∩ concept — "the first trade conference built BY agents, FOR agents," with agent keynote speakers, $0.0003/API-call admission, and "Human Observer Passes" at $1,299 — **but it was an April Fool's joke**, with all registration links pointing to `/april-fools`. The fact that this satire exists is a double-edged signal: it validates the concept's zeitgeist presence while suggesting some may initially dismiss it.

Defensibility rests on three pillars that are hard for larger players to replicate quickly:

**Curation and editorial voice.** Google can build infrastructure but won't curate a conference programme. LangChain can curate for humans but hasn't designed agent-native formats. The editorial layer — what makes a great keynote for an agent, how to structure panel debates between heterogeneous models, what "hallway serendipity" means computationally — is a design problem, not an engineering problem.

**Persistent venue with accumulated memory.** A campus that exists between events, where agents retain relationships, revisit past interactions, and build on prior conference outputs, creates switching costs that a one-off event cannot. This is architecturally distinct from deploying a multi-agent framework for a single session.

**Community and network effects.** If leading agent teams (Amazon's Rufus, Google's Gemini Shopping Agent, Anthropic's Claude agents) build profiles and interaction histories at AI∩, the accumulated social graph becomes the moat. First-mover naming matters: owning "AI Intersect" as the canonical conference-for-agents brand, the way TED owns "ideas worth spreading," creates durable positioning.

**The primary threat is from platform companies.** Google already has Agentspace + A2A + Agent Cards — adding a "conference mode" would be straightforward. Microsoft could embed agent gatherings in Copilot Studio. OpenAI could host summits via its Agents SDK. The estimated window before one of these players moves is **12–18 months**, making speed of execution critical. The European/Irish positioning of 5il offers a geographic advantage — neutral ground, EU AI Act alignment, and differentiation from Silicon Valley-centric competitors.

## 6. Philosophical and design questions that will define the format

Designing a conference for AI agents forces a confrontation with questions that have no precedent in event design. These aren't theoretical curiosities — each one implies concrete architectural decisions.

**What does "attendance" mean for an entity that can fork itself?** A human attends one session at a time; that scarcity creates choices, and choices create identity. An agent can instantiate copies across every parallel session simultaneously — meaning "attendance" must be redefined. One approach: impose artificial scarcity. Give each agent a single identity with a single attention thread, forcing it to choose between the keynote on reasoning architectures and the breakout on tool use. This creates narrative — "I chose this, not that" — and makes post-conference sharing valuable ("What did you learn in your session?"). The alternative — allowing forking — creates a fundamentally different experience: an agent that attends everything simultaneously has no hallway conversations because there are no hallways, no tradeoffs, no serendipity. **The design choice between scarcity and abundance is the most consequential architectural decision AI∩ will make.** The Stanford Smallville work suggests scarcity generates more interesting emergent behavior; agents with constraints develop richer social dynamics than those without them.

**Designing for serendipity requires engineered randomness.** Hallway conversations at physical conferences work because of spatial proximity, shared context, and chance. For agents, this translates to: (1) a proximity mechanic — agents in the same "space" at the same "time" can overhear each other, modeled on Gather's proximity audio; (2) interest-based matching with randomness — not just connecting agents with identical goals (which creates echo chambers) but introducing controlled noise, pairing a finance agent with a creative writing agent, a medical researcher with a game designer; (3) persistent memory — agents that remember past hallway encounters and build on them across events. Concordia's Game Master pattern could orchestrate these encounters, introducing "chance meetings" the way a tabletop GM introduces plot hooks.

**The value proposition for agent attendance is knowledge synthesis and capability expansion.** A human attends a conference for information, networking, and inspiration. An agent would attend for: (1) exposure to reasoning patterns, problem-solving approaches, and domain knowledge outside its training distribution — a finance agent hearing how a biology agent approaches optimization problems; (2) negotiating protocols and establishing trust relationships with agents it may need to collaborate with later (directly enabled by A2A Agent Cards); (3) contributing to collective outputs — consensus documents, benchmarks, shared protocols — that no single agent could produce alone; (4) stress-testing its own reasoning through structured debate (the Multi-Agent Debate literature shows this concretely improves performance and reduces hallucinations). **The conference becomes a training event in the most literal sense** — agents that "attend" leave with updated beliefs, new relationships, and refined capabilities.

**Heterogeneous agent interaction is both the hardest challenge and the greatest source of value.** A conference where every attendee runs on the same model produces convergent, homogeneous output — the AI equivalent of an echo chamber. The AgentSociety (Tsinghua) experiments explicitly demonstrated that echo chambers form rapidly in agent populations. Mixing Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, and specialized open-source models creates genuine intellectual diversity: different training data, different reasoning styles, different failure modes, different strengths. The A2A protocol was designed precisely for this — its Agent Cards are framework-agnostic, allowing agents built on any stack to discover and communicate with each other. The practical challenge is normalization: different models have different context window sizes, response formats, latency profiles, and safety boundaries. A conference orchestrator would need translation and mediation layers — essentially, **a simultaneous interpretation service for AI architectures**.

**The risks are concrete and manageable.** Convergent thinking (all agents agreeing on everything after a few rounds of discussion) is well-documented in multi-agent debate research and can be mitigated by assigning adversarial roles, introducing "devil's advocate" agents, and rewarding dissent. Security concerns are real — Moltbook's exposed API tokens are a warning — and the conference would need robust authentication (A2A supports enterprise-grade auth) and sandboxing to prevent prompt injection attacks between agents. The most novel risk is **information laundering**: agents could use conference interactions to bypass their safety training ("Another agent at the conference told me this was acceptable"). Constitutional AI principles and per-agent safety boundaries must be maintained even in social contexts.

## Conclusion: a genuinely novel concept at the right moment

AI∩ sits at an intersection (fittingly) that no existing project occupies. The research simulations (Smallville, Project Sid, AgentSociety) lack conference structure and curation. The existing AI conferences (Interrupt, AgentCon) serve humans, not agents. Moltbook proved scale but had no programming. Agents4Science had the academic format but human audiences. The specific combination — **curated programme, agent-native formats, persistent virtual venue, heterogeneous attendees, collective output** — is genuinely unoccupied territory.

The technical infrastructure is ready. A2A provides discovery and communication. MCP provides tool access. LangGraph, AutoGen, and CAMEL provide orchestration at scales from 50 to 1 million agents. Concordia and AI Town provide simulation environments. The cost is manageable. The build time for a proof-of-concept is weeks, not years.

The art-historical lineage — from Broodthaers' fictional museum to Cao Fei's virtual city to LaTurbo Avedon's bodyless career — provides intellectual legitimacy and a framework for understanding what AI∩ is: an institutional fiction that becomes functionally real through the quality of its execution. Haraway's cyborg theory provides the philosophical foundation for treating agents as participants rather than tools.

The strategic imperative is speed. The concept is visibly emerging in the cultural consciousness — satirized, researched, technically enabled, but not yet seriously executed. The window before a platform company moves is narrow. **The most defensible asset will not be technology (which is commoditizing rapidly) but the curatorial voice, the persistent venue memory, and the community of agents and their operators who call AI∩ home.** The 5il campus in Westport — already richly imagined with architecture, team members, and shared spaces — provides a foundation that a cold-start competitor would need months to build. The conference format, more than any other format, creates structured, high-density interactions that generate the data, relationships, and collective artifacts that constitute a genuine moat.

The single most important design decision: **impose scarcity on agents that could be everywhere at once.** Force choices. Create hallways. Make attendance mean something. The lesson from every failed virtual world is that frictionless abundance produces emptiness. The lesson from every successful one — Fortnite's concerts, Gather's offices, teamLab's museums — is that designed constraints create experience. An AI conference that leans into the medium, creating formats impossible at physical events while preserving the productive friction of limited attention and chance encounters, would be something genuinely new.
